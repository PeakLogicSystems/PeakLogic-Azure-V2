An intelligence layer for essential field services

PeakLogic connects the equipment your business already operates and services — pumps, treatment units, chemistry, refrigeration — to monitoring, alerts, automation, and dispatch. Issues surface early, and the right work lands in the right system. Asset visibility and risk reduction, delivered as a service.

Why essential-service operators cannot keep running blind

Essential services do not get to fail quietly. When a lift station overflows, a treatment unit stops disinfecting, or a walk-in loses temperature, the outcome is a regulator, a public health notice, or a customer lost — not a maintenance ticket.

Yet these are the assets nobody can see: too dispersed for a control room, too small for SCADA economics, and maintained by crews that shrink while the estate they cover grows. The gap between what an operator is accountable for and what they can actually observe widens every year — and every one of those failures announced itself first, in current draw, in run time, in drifting chemistry.

PeakLogic closes that gap without asking anyone to become a SCADA operator. It reads the warning, decides what matters, and puts the work in front of the person who can act on it.

What the platform actually does

Four steps. Most competitors stop after the first, which is why an alarm reaches a phone and nothing else happens.

01 · Monitor

Connect what exists

Pumps, blowers, chemistry, level, refrigeration — mixed vendors, on one platform, over cellular or the site's own network.

02 · Predict

Read the warning

Per-asset baselines catch degradation — rising draw, longer cycles, drifting chemistry — weeks before the failure it becomes.

03 · Dispatch

Land the work

An alarm becomes a work order in the servicing partner's own system, routed to whoever is accountable, not an email nobody owns.

04 · Prove

Keep the evidence

The record a regulator, insurer or disputing customer asks for is compiled as it happens, not reconstructed from paper afterwards.

Positioning

For operators of distributed critical equipment — wastewater and septic, campus and assisted-living facilities, food service, pools, and light commercial mechanical — who cannot justify a traditional SCADA deployment but cannot absorb the failures either, PeakLogic is a predictive analytics and asset-management platform that converts unplanned failure into scheduled work.

Autodialers call once the overflow has happened. SCADA is priced and scoped as a capital project. PeakLogic reaches these sites a third way — through the service contractors already maintaining them — so it arrives with no integration project and pays for itself out of emergencies that no longer occur.

How it reaches the market

The distribution model is the part of this business that is genuinely difficult to copy.

The constraint is truck rolls, not demand

Every competitor's growth is capped by how many sites their own people can drive to. We remove that cap by making the service contractor the channel, so customer acquisition costs a partner signature rather than a site visit. One partner with 90 sites is worth ninety direct sales — and they install on visits already scheduled.

Direct sales run alongside it

Some operators run their own crews, and institutional campuses have no contractor in the middle. Direct is the smaller book by site count but the higher-value one per site, and it produces the reference customers that make a channel conversation credible. Roughly 39% of recurring revenue is direct across the plan.

The problem, and what it costs

Across wastewater, septic, pools, HVAC, and cold chain the operating model is identical: assets run until they fail or somebody complains. The cost is not the repair — it is the consequence.

Why now — and why the window is dated

Four forces, ranked by how much of the market each one moves. Only the second is time-boxed — but it is the one that determines whether this is the right twenty-four months.

01
Market creation, not displacement

At most of these sites, the incumbent is nothing at all

"Displacement" overstates it. The overwhelming majority of lift stations, treatment units, and light commercial mechanical rooms have no monitoring at all, or a phone autodialer that calls once the failure has happened. There is no vendor to unseat, no contract to buy out, and nothing to rip out.

That is the stronger position: no switching friction, no incumbent defending an account, and a buyer adopting for the first time rather than being asked to admit an earlier decision was wrong. Where we do displace, it is narrow — ageing autodialers, and OEM apps that see only their own manufacturer's equipment.

02
Time-boxed

Consolidation has orphaned the installer channel

DataFlow Systems, the dominant lift-station monitoring vendor, was acquired by SmartCover in 2023 and SmartCover by Badger Meter in 2025 for $185M. The product line is being folded into Badger's BlueEdge suite: contracts, billing, support contacts, and domains are all moving, and small-to-midsize lift-station work receives the least attention during an integration of that size.

This is both a displacement and a partnership opening, and the partnership half is the durable one. Panel builders are the distribution channel, not the competition. They already sell to towns and operators, most of their panels still ship with a dialer or nothing, and they want recurring revenue without building cloud software. This window closes when Badger finishes integrating — the channel relationships formed during the disruption are what outlast it.

03
Structural

A modernisation cycle is arriving whether operators are ready or not

Three forces are converging on one estate. The installed base of dialers and custom relay panels is reaching end of life just as the cellular and sensing hardware to replace it has fallen to a fraction of its former cost. Meanwhile the workforce that carried this knowledge in their heads is retiring, and the crews replacing them are smaller and cover more sites.

The result is an operator being asked to supervise a growing estate with fewer people and ageing instrumentation — and increasingly being asked by their own board, insurer or council to evidence that they can. Modernisation stops being discretionary at that point. The question becomes what they modernise onto.

04
Now a procurement gate

Security has moved from a checkbox to a condition of sale

The 2026 coordinated intrusions against US water utilities changed what a security review is for. Legacy autodialers and one-off control panels have essentially no security posture to present: no patching story, no audit trail, no way to answer what a compromised device could reach. Operators are now being asked those questions by regulators, insurers, and their own boards, and many cannot answer them.

Our position here is a commercial asset rather than an engineering detail, and it has two parts. Today the platform is read-only by construction — no code path issues a device command and no permission grants one. For a utility security reviewer that is a materially shorter conversation than any competitor can offer, and it removes the single largest objection to connecting a plant to anything.

Control is on the roadmap, because operators will eventually want remote reset, setpoint change and shutoff, and a monitoring-only product would cede that ground. The advantage is not that we will never actuate — it is the order in which we get to build. The authorisation model, the safety interlocks, and the audit trail are being designed before a single command path exists. Safety-critical actions stay local to the equipment and trip on their own sensor reading without waiting on the cloud; the platform provides override, evidence, and accountability rather than the trip itself.

Every incumbent in this category is attempting the reverse — retrofitting a security posture onto control systems designed before any of this was a threat. That is substantially harder, and it is why a security review is a competitive event for us rather than an obstacle.

Drivers 3 and 4 are the ones that make this a market rather than a niche — they apply to every site in it, not just those a competitor happens to be neglecting. Driver 2 is what makes the next twenty-four months the right ones to move in.

Why the partner, and not the site owner

A site owner buys monitoring once, reluctantly, and churns when budgets tighten. A service company buys it to protect margin on contracts they already have — fewer emergency callouts, fewer wasted visits, evidence when a customer disputes work. That is a operating-cost decision, not a capital one, and it renews because the alternative is going back to driving blind.

Revenue mix, and why gross profit is the better measure

Revenue is hardware-heavy in the early years — only 21% of Year 3 revenue is recurring, because every new site carries ~$1,800 of equipment. Presented as SaaS, that would be misleading, and it is exactly the flaw we found in the prior forecasts.

So we report gross profit mix instead of revenue mix. Hardware runs at ~20% margin and software at ~82%, which means recurring gross profit overtakes hardware in Year 2 and reaches 60% by Year 5, 81% by Year 8. Revenue makes this look like an equipment business; profit shows it is not.

The channel asset

An affiliated operating company — a septic and wastewater service business with an established regional fleet — provides the first partner deployment without a sales cycle. This converts the hardest problem in the plan (proving the model on real sites, with real technicians, against real equipment) into an internal scheduling exercise. It is the single largest de-risking factor in this proposal, and no competitor entering this market has an equivalent.

How the figure was sized

Cash is modelled monthly and the investment sized against the deepest trough — $819K in month 18 — plus a buffer. Costs land from month one while recurring revenue compounds late; an annual average would hide the point at which the company actually runs out of money.

Why $1.05M funds $1.13M of cost. Operating cost over 24 months is $1.13M; revenue earned in those months contributes $259K of gross profit. Capital is needed only for the gap, and only down to its deepest point — covered here with roughly 28% headroom.

The team is two senior roles — platform and engineering — rising to nine by year five. Development is AI-assisted and partners perform installation, so headcount tracks partner count rather than site count. Staffing an installation team would rebuild the constraint this model exists to remove.

Why a strategic buyer pays it

This category is actively consolidating and the comparables are recent and public: SmartCover acquired DataFlow Systems (2023); Badger Meter acquired SmartCover for $185M (2025). Xylem and Veralto are acquiring in adjacent water-infrastructure software.

What those buyers are acquiring is distribution into the long tail — the hundreds of thousands of small sites their enterprise products cannot economically reach. A platform arriving with a signed contractor channel, several thousand monitored sites and a direct reference book is that distribution, already assembled. It is materially harder to build than to buy, which is what supports the upper half of the ranges above.
